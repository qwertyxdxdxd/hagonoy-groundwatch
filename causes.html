Causes & Effects

Why the ground in Hagonoy is sinking, and what that means for floods, water, and infrastructure.

Main cause: Groundwater extraction

Groundwater is water stored underground in layers of sand and gravel called aquifers. In Bulacan and neighboring provinces, deep wells have been a major source of water for homes, commercial establishments, factories, and fishponds.

The problem begins when extraction is greater than recharge, meaning more water is pumped out than rain and rivers can replace. The water table drops, clay layers compact, and the land sinks. Researchers studying northern Manila Bay have linked the fast subsidence in coastal Bulacan and Pampanga to this kind of groundwater over-pumping (Rodolfo & Siringan, 2006).

Who uses groundwater?

  • Households using private deep wells and jetmatic or electric pumps
  • Water districts and subdivisions that rely on production wells
  • Fishponds and aquaculture operations
  • Commercial establishments, ice plants, and industries

Contributing factors

  • Unregulated or unregistered wells. Many private wells operate without permits, so total extraction is hard to monitor.
  • Population growth. More households mean more demand for water.
  • Limited surface-water supply. Where piped water from rivers and dams is not available, people turn to wells.
  • Saltwater intrusion. As shallow wells turn salty, deeper wells are dug, and this pulls water from deeper layers.
  • Loading on soft soil. Filling and raising roads and buildings adds weight that compresses the ground further.

Effects of land subsidence

Flooding

Deeper and longer floods

As the ground sinks, more areas fall below high-tide level. Places that once flooded only during typhoons now flood during ordinary high tides ("baha kahit walang ulan", flooding even without rain). Floodwater also drains more slowly, because rivers and canals lose the slope they need to flow out to the bay.

Infrastructure

Damage to roads, homes, and bridges

  • Roads must be raised again and again, which leaves houses below street level
  • Cracked walls, floors, and foundations from uneven sinking
  • Bridges with reduced clearance, and drainage pipes that break or reverse flow
  • Well casings that appear to "rise" out of the ground as the land sinks around them
Water

Saltwater intrusion

When fresh groundwater is over-pumped near the coast, seawater can move into the aquifer. Wells become salty and unusable for drinking or farming, which pushes people to dig deeper and makes subsidence even worse.

Environment

Loss of land and habitats

Low-lying land, fishpond dikes, and mangrove areas can become permanently submerged. Coastal ecosystems that protect against storm surges are lost.

Economy

Rising costs

Families spend repeatedly on raising floors, repairing homes, and replacing appliances. The government spends on road raising, dikes, and pumping stations, and businesses lose working days to floods.

Long term

Mostly irreversible

Compacted clay does not recover. Elevation lost to subsidence is effectively permanent, which is why prevention and reducing groundwater use matter so much (Galloway et al., 1999).

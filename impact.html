Community Impact

Land subsidence is not just a scientific problem. It changes how families in Hagonoy live, work, and learn.

Living with sinking ground

In many parts of Hagonoy, flooding has become a part of daily life. Residents adapt by building raised floors, moving belongings to second stories, and using boats or wading through water to get to school and work. These adaptations help, but they carry real costs to people's health, income, and wellbeing.

Households

  • Repeated spending to raise floors, rebuild walls, and elevate homes
  • Homes ending up below the level of newly raised roads, so they collect water
  • Damaged furniture, appliances, and important documents
  • Some families forced to relocate as their land becomes permanently wet

Livelihoods

  • Fishpond operators: dikes overtopped by tides, and fish escaping during floods
  • Farmers: salty soil and water reduce harvests
  • Small businesses: lost working days, damaged stock, and fewer customers during floods
  • Workers: longer, harder commutes through flooded roads

Education

  • Class suspensions during floods and high tides
  • Flooded classrooms and damaged learning materials
  • Students wading through floodwater to reach school
  • Schools needing raised floors and buildings

Health and safety

  • Waterborne and flood-related diseases such as leptospirosis, diarrhea, and skin infections
  • More breeding sites for mosquitoes that carry dengue
  • Unsafe drinking water when wells become salty or contaminated
  • Risk of injury and drowning during floods
  • Stress and anxiety from constant flooding

Public infrastructure

  • Roads, bridges, and drainage that need constant repair or raising
  • Churches, barangay halls, and markets affected by flooding
  • Higher public spending on flood control and pumping stations

Culture and heritage

  • Older structures and heritage sites at risk of water damage
  • Town fiestas and community events disrupted by floods
  • A shift in identity as coastal barangays become "water villages"

Timeline: How the problem has grown

Subsidence builds up slowly, but the record of floods, research, and policy shows how the problem has grown around northern Manila Bay. Use the buttons to filter by type.

  1. 1970s–1980sTrend

    Groundwater use expands

    Growing towns, fishponds, and industries around northern Manila Bay rely more and more on deep wells for their water.

  2. 1976Law

    Water Code of the Philippines (P.D. 1067)

    Requires permits for water use, including groundwater extraction, and sets the rules the NWRB enforces today (Presidential Decree No. 1067, 1976).

  3. 1989Law

    Rainwater Collector Act (R.A. 6716)

    Provides for rainwater collectors and spring development in barangays, an early alternative to pumping groundwater (Republic Act No. 6716, 1989).

  4. 1990s–2000sTrend

    Tidal flooding becomes routine

    Coastal communities in Bulacan and Pampanga increasingly see high tides flood streets and homes on days without rain. Roads and floors are raised again and again.

  5. 2004Law

    Philippine Clean Water Act (R.A. 9275)

    Strengthens protection of the country's water resources and water quality (Republic Act No. 9275, 2004).

  6. 2006Research

    Subsidence identified as the hidden cause

    Rodolfo and Siringan show that worsening floods around northern Manila Bay are driven largely by human-caused land subsidence, not only by global sea-level rise (Rodolfo & Siringan, 2006).

  7. 2009Flood

    Typhoons Ondoy and Pepeng

    Back-to-back typhoons (international names Ketsana and Parma) bring widespread flooding to Metro Manila and Central Luzon.

  8. 2011Flood

    Typhoons Pedring and Quiel

    Typhoons Pedring (Nesat) and Quiel (Nalgae) leave large parts of Bulacan and Pampanga, including Hagonoy, under floodwater, in many areas for days to weeks.

  9. 2012Flood

    Southwest monsoon (Habagat) floods

    Days of enhanced monsoon rain in August flood Metro Manila and nearby provinces, including Bulacan.

  10. 2013Research

    Satellites measure the sinking

    Raucoules and colleagues use satellite radar (InSAR) to measure fast ground movement in the Manila area from 1993 to 2010 (Raucoules et al., 2013).

  11. 2019Program

    Manila Bay rehabilitation

    The national government launches a large-scale program to clean and rehabilitate Manila Bay and its coastal areas.

  12. 2021Research

    Subsidence as a global threat

    A study in the journal Science maps land subsidence worldwide and identifies coastal and delta areas like ours as high-risk (Herrera-García et al., 2021).

  13. 2023Flood

    Typhoon Egay and the enhanced Habagat

    Typhoon Egay (Doksuri) and heavy monsoon rains in July cause severe, long-lasting flooding across Bulacan and Pampanga.

  14. 2024Flood

    Typhoon Carina and the enhanced Habagat

    Typhoon Carina (Gaemi) and monsoon rains in July bring major flooding to Metro Manila and Bulacan.

  15. TodayTrend

    Sinking land plus rising seas

    With land still subsiding and sea level rising, every typhoon and high tide reaches further into the town. Slowing subsidence now protects Hagonoy's future.

Typhoon names are local PAGASA names, with international names in parentheses.

Reflection: What does this look like in your barangay?

Think about your street, school, or home. Has the road been raised? Does high tide reach places it didn't before? Has your family had to elevate your floor? These everyday observations are signs of subsidence.
